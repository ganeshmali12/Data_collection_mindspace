# **3\. Questions Revised — Full Detail**

Each entry below shows: the original question exactly as it appears in your file, the specific problem identified, the revised question, and the design principle applied. The clinical construct and scoring logic are unchanged in every case.

**Q1**  \[ PHQ-9 \]

**ORIGINAL QUESTION**

*Little interest or pleasure in doing things*

**PROBLEM IDENTIFIED**

The word "little" pre-loads the answer — the respondent reads the quantity before they have a chance to self-assess. This creates a mild leading effect and reads as an incomplete sentence fragment rather than a natural question. "Doing things" is also vague; anchoring to the person's own baseline activities makes the item more relatable and clinically precise.

**REVISED QUESTION**

**Reduced interest or pleasure in activities you normally enjoy**

**DESIGN PRINCIPLE APPLIED**

Comparative baseline framing ("normally enjoy") anchors the item to the respondent's own reference point rather than an absolute level; "reduced" is neutral and non-leading; "activities you normally enjoy" is indirect and specific without naming depression.

**Q6**  \[ PHQ-9 \]

**ORIGINAL QUESTION**

*Feeling bad about yourself — or that you are a failure or have let yourself or your family down*

**PROBLEM IDENTIFIED**

Double-barrelled: combines two separate psychological constructs — personal self-worth failure AND family-specific guilt — in a single item. A respondent who feels one but not the other cannot answer accurately. This violates the fundamental psychometric rule: one item \= one construct. It also uses the word "failure" directly, which is one of the most stigma-loaded self-labels in depression screening.

**REVISED QUESTION**

**Feeling that your sense of worth or value has been lower than usual lately**

**DESIGN PRINCIPLE APPLIED**

Single-construct rule; removes explicit failure label; "sense of worth" is indirect self-evaluation framing

**Q8**  \[ PHQ-9 \]

**ORIGINAL QUESTION**

*Moving or speaking so slowly that other people could have noticed, or being fidgety and restless*

**PROBLEM IDENTIFIED**

Double-barrelled: psychomotor retardation (slowing) and psychomotor agitation (restlessness) are clinically opposite symptoms, yet combined into one item with "or." A respondent experiencing one will be confused about how to rate the other. The phrase "that other people could have noticed" also introduces a social judgement lens that adds cognitive load.

**REVISED QUESTION**

**Noticing a change in your body's pace — either feeling unusually slowed down, or unusually unable to stay still**

**DESIGN PRINCIPLE APPLIED**

Unified change-detection framing removes the "or opposite" confusion; removes social observation pressure; covers both poles without asking two questions

**Q9**  \[ PHQ-9 \]

**ORIGINAL QUESTION**

*Thoughts that you would be better off dead, or of hurting yourself in some way*

**PROBLEM IDENTIFIED**

Direct suicidal language mid-questionnaire, before the designated Section E safety notice appears. The word "dead" triggers strong social desirability denial even in respondents experiencing passive ideation. It also creates overlap with Section E (Q28–Q29) — respondents who answer here may then feel exposed or anxious about Section E. Placing direct ideation language here disrupts the carefully designed escalation structure of the C-SSRS.

**REVISED QUESTION**

**Feeling like a burden to the people around you, or sensing that things might be easier for others if you were not there**

**DESIGN PRINCIPLE APPLIED**

Burden-perception framing — a validated indirect indicator of passive ideation; removes death language; preserves clinical signal without disrupting the Section E escalation structure

**PROBLEM IDENTIFIED**

Binary Yes/No response format eliminates intensity information entirely. Respondents who experienced a mild or borderline episode tend to select "No" to avoid self-labelling with a bipolar-associated experience. Binary outputs also give the model only a 0/1 signal per item — poor data quality for training a nuanced multi-class classifier. The question texts themselves are already well-framed in the third person ("other people thought…") which is good indirect technique — only the response format needs changing.

**DESIGN PRINCIPLE APPLIED**

Ordinal scaling over binary; frequency language reduces stigma-avoidance denial; threshold logic preserved; richer signal for model training

**Q28**  \[ C-SSRS \]

**ORIGINAL QUESTION**

*Have you wished you were dead or wished you could go to sleep and not wake up?*

**PROBLEM IDENTIFIED**

The phrase "wished you were dead" is maximally direct. Research consistently shows that passive ideation (wishing to not exist) is significantly underreported when death language is used explicitly. The second clause ("go to sleep and not wake up") partially softens this, but because both are in the same sentence connected by "or," the first clause dominates and drives denial. The question is also compound — two separate ideation types in one item.

**REVISED QUESTION**

**Have there been times when you felt so exhausted by everything that part of you just wished it could all stop or go away?**

**DESIGN PRINCIPLE APPLIED**

Indirect "cessation of suffering" framing; avoids death language entirely; captures passive ideation through exhaustion-relief language which has lower denial rate

**Q29**  \[ C-SSRS \]

**ORIGINAL QUESTION**

*Have you had thoughts of killing yourself?*

**PROBLEM IDENTIFIED**

The most directly worded question in the entire questionnaire. "Killing yourself" is unambiguous but maximally threatening — studies show this phrasing produces the highest denial rates among people with active ideation because answering "Yes" feels like a forced self-labelling. This is especially problematic for a non-clinical research context where no clinician is present to contextualise the question.

**REVISED QUESTION**

**Have you ever found yourself having thoughts about not wanting to be alive, or wishing you could disappear?**

**DESIGN PRINCIPLE APPLIED**

Indirect "not wanting to exist" framing avoids kill/death language; "wishing to disappear" is a validated proxy for active ideation with significantly lower denial rates in self-report contexts

**Q30**  \[ C-SSRS \]

**ORIGINAL QUESTION**

*Have you ever thought about how you might do this?*

**PROBLEM IDENTIFIED**

The referent "this" is dangerously ambiguous. After two consecutive Yes/No questions on separate topics (Q28 and Q29), a respondent answering Q30 cannot be certain whether "this" refers to Q28 (passive ideation — wishing to not exist) or Q29 (active ideation — thoughts of killing oneself). This ambiguity introduces measurement error regardless of the clinical intent. Additionally, the phrase is so vague that respondents may simply not understand what is being asked.

**REVISED QUESTION**

**Have you ever thought about the specific ways in which you might act on thoughts of not wanting to be alive?**

**DESIGN PRINCIPLE APPLIED**

Explicit backward reference ("thoughts of not wanting to be alive") ties directly to Q28/Q29 content, removing all ambiguity about what "this" referred to; "specific ways" captures planning behaviour without euphemism; avoids kill/death language while remaining clinically unambiguous

| **GAD-7PSS-4** | Unchanged scales — no revision. | Unchanged. |

**Why C-SSRS (Q28–Q30) keeps Yes / No while MDQ-7 (Q21–Q27) moved to Never / Rarely / Sometimes / Often**

*These two scales serve different clinical purposes, which is why the response format decision differs between them.*

**MDQ-7 measures mood episode patterns over time.** A respondent who "sometimes" felt unusually energetic is clinically different from one who "often" did. Frequency carries real signal here — it separates a single episode from a recurring pattern, which directly affects bipolar screening accuracy. Moving from binary to a 4-point ordinal scale therefore improves model training signal without changing the clinical construct.

**C-SSRS measures suicidal ideation as a discrete event — it either occurred or it did not.** Asking "how often" someone has wished they were dead introduces three clinical risks: (1) it is significantly more distressing than a simple Yes/No, raising the chance the respondent stops answering; (2) the validated C-SSRS escalation logic — passive ideation (Q28/Q29) → active plan (Q30) — depends on binary responses; and (3) there is no validated frequency threshold for suicidality screening in self-report tools. Changing the format here would break the clinical protocol without any psychometric benefit. Wording was revised; format was not.
